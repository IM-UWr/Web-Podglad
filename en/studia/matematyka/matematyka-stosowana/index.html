About

Applied mathematics describes real-world phenomena in the language of mathematics: how a population grows, how an epidemic spreads, how a chemical substance diffuses, or how patterns form in reaction–diffusion systems. In this specialisation you learn to build such models — deterministic ones (based on differential equations) and random ones (based on stochastic processes) — to study their properties, and then to solve them numerically and test them in computer simulations.

Classes combine theory with work at the computer. In Deterministic modelling you move from the Malthus model and the logistic equation, through the Lotka–Volterra model and the SIR epidemic model, to the diffusion equation, travelling waves and Turing instability, and analyse the solutions numerically in the laboratory. Stochastic modelling introduces Markov chains, the Poisson process, renewal processes and Brownian motion; Numerical analysis covers computational algorithms; and in the second cycle Introduction to simulation and Monte Carlo methods teaches you to generate pseudorandom numbers and simulate random processes. The programme is complemented by statistics, databases, machine learning and time series.

The specialisation is closely linked to the Department of Differential Equations, where its coordinator, Prof. Grzegorz Karch, works. The department studies nonlinear partial differential equations and their applications in biology and physics — including models of chemotaxis and pattern formation and equations with nonlocal diffusion — and runs the open “Differential Equations” seminar. Projects carried out at the Institute include the European doctoral programme “Mathematical Methods in Natural Sciences”, led by Prof. Piotr Biler and Prof. Grzegorz Karch, and the NCN project “Mathematical analysis of models of bacterial colony formation”. According to the USOS catalogue, members of the department also teach key courses of the specialisation, such as Differential equations 1 R, Differential equations 2 R and Deterministic modelling, and on the list of thesis topics they propose topics on epidemic models, chemotaxis and bacterial colonies.

What sets applied mathematics apart from the other specialisations is its focus on models of natural phenomena and the methods for computing them. Only this specialisation has Classical mechanics (first cycle) and courses in the foundations of the exact or natural sciences (NP category, second cycle) as compulsory elements, and — as in theoretical mathematics — it requires the extended version of Differential equations 1 R. The specialisation is offered in both first- and second-cycle studies.

First-cycle studies

In the bachelor’s programme, the plans of the data analysis, actuarial and financial mathematics, applied mathematics and theoretical mathematics specialisations are very similar in semesters 1–4, so you make the final decision at the end of semester 4. It is worth thinking about it from semester 2, though: applied mathematics requires Differential equations 1 R (the extended version) and an English language course. You take the courses typical of the specialisation in your third year: Deterministic modelling, Classical mechanics, Numerical analysis and Databases in semester 5, and Stochastic modelling and Machine learning in semester 6. First-cycle studies end with a written diploma examination (there is no bachelor’s thesis).

Second-cycle studies

In the master’s programme you declare your specialisation at the beginning of the first semester. The compulsory specialisation courses are Introduction to simulation and Monte Carlo methods, Differential equations 2 R, Functional analysis 1, Advanced numerical methods, Time series and the Specialisation team project. In addition, you take two survey seminars, an NP course (at least 4 ECTS) and mathematical electives — at least 36 ECTS in categories M or T and 11 ECTS in categories L, M or T. The specialisation assumes knowledge of the material of Differential equations 1 R, Deterministic modelling, Stochastic modelling and Numerical analysis. Your studies end with a master’s thesis and a diploma examination: the written part covers the compulsory specialisation courses and the oral part covers the topic of your thesis.

Natural science courses (NP)

A distinctive feature of the specialisation are courses in the foundations of the natural or exact sciences other than mathematics and computer science — biology, chemistry, physics, earth and environmental sciences or astronomy. The Mathematical Institute regularly offers Fundamentals of genetics and Geostatistical methods in environmental analysis, but you may — and are in fact encouraged to — choose a course at any faculty of the University of Wrocław. You then need the consent of the dean of the faculty teaching the course and the consent of the Dean of the Faculty of Mathematics and Computer Science to count it in the NP category. The Dean decides after consulting the specialisation coordinator, so agree on your choice with the coordinator first.

Previous curriculum

The plan described above applies to students who started their studies in October 2026 or later. Students who started in February 2026 or earlier follow the previous curriculum (UWr Senate resolution no. 40/2022). Under that curriculum, an applied mathematics student must earn at least 6 ECTS for NP courses in the first cycle and another 6 ECTS in the second cycle; the compulsory second-cycle courses of the previous curriculum are listed under “What you will learn”.

Who is it for

For those who enjoy differential equations and probability, want to combine mathematics with programming and computation, and are interested in applications of mathematics in the natural sciences — from population dynamics and epidemics to physics and earth sciences.

Why choose it

  • Models of real phenomenaYou model population growth, the spread of epidemics, diffusion and pattern formation using ordinary and partial differential equations.
  • From equations to simulationsNumerical analysis, advanced numerical methods, Monte Carlo simulations and Python programming — you do not just analyse models, you also compute them.
  • Randomness and dataStochastic modelling, time series, statistics, databases and machine learning complement deterministic models with random phenomena and working with data.
  • Natural sciences in the programmeClassical mechanics and, in the second cycle, a course in biology, chemistry, physics or earth sciences — which you can even take at another UWr faculty.
  • Close to researchThe coordinator is Prof. Grzegorz Karch of the Department of Differential Equations, which studies, among others, models of chemotaxis, pattern formation and nonlocal diffusion.
  • TeamworkThe team project in the first cycle and the specialisation team project in the second cycle teach you to work together on a mathematical problem and present the results.

How the specialisation works

  1. Semesters 1–4

    Common foundations

    Analysis, algebra, Python programming, topology, measure and integral, probability. In semester 4 you take Differential equations 1 R (the extended version, compulsory in this specialisation) and Fundamentals of practical statistics.

  2. End of semester 4

    Declaring the specialisation

    By 30 June you submit Form C2. The plans of the AD, MAF, MS and theoretical mathematics specialisations are similar in semesters 1–4, so you can make the final decision only now.

  3. Semester 5

    Modelling and computation

    Deterministic modelling, Classical mechanics, Numerical analysis and Databases.

  4. Semester 6

    Randomness, data and the bachelor’s examination

    Stochastic modelling, Machine learning and electives. After completing semester 6 you take the written diploma examination (no thesis is required in the first cycle).

  5. Second cycle — semesters 1–2

    Declaration and specialisation courses

    By 15 October you submit Form C3. You take Introduction to simulation and Monte Carlo methods, Differential equations 2 R, Functional analysis 1 and Advanced numerical methods; Master’s seminar 1 helps you choose a thesis topic.

  6. Second cycle — semesters 3–4

    Master’s thesis and examination

    At the beginning of semester 3 you register your supervisor and thesis topic (Form E). Time series, the Specialisation team project, Master’s seminar 2; finally the diploma examination — written on the compulsory specialisation courses and oral on the topic of your thesis.

What you will learn

Specialisation courses — first cycle (curriculum from 2026/27)

  • Introduction to combinatoricsSemester 1, 2 ECTS.
  • Topology (standard / extended)Semester 2, 5/6 ECTS; you choose the version. Foundations of general topology and metric spaces and various types of convergence of sequences of functions — preparation for, among others, probability theory and functional analysis.
  • Measure and integral (standard / extended)Semester 3, 5/6 ECTS; you choose the version. Lebesgue measure, measurable functions, the integral and limit theorems, Fubini’s theorem.
  • Programming 2 (Python)Semester 3, 6 ECTS. Basics of algorithms and computational complexity, data structures (including graph-based ones), object-oriented programming and design.
  • Differential equations 1 (extended)Semester 4, 7 ECTS; the extended version is compulsory in this specialisation. Basic concepts, methods and theorems of ordinary differential equations, elements of partial differential equations and first mathematical models based on differential equations.
  • Fundamentals of practical statisticsSemester 4, 7 ECTS. Practical methods of statistical analysis — descriptive statistics, estimation and hypothesis testing. It can be replaced by Statistics (semester 5) or Fundamentals of statistics (semester 6).
  • DatabasesSemester 5, 6 ECTS. Relational databases and SQL — working in a specific system (e.g. PostgreSQL) and designing a database on your own.
  • Deterministic modellingSemester 5, 6 ECTS (lecture and laboratory). Building models based on differential equations: from the Malthus and Lotka–Volterra models, through epidemic models (SIR), to the diffusion equation, travelling waves and Turing instability — with numerical analysis of the solutions.
  • Classical mechanicsSemester 5, 6 ECTS.
  • Numerical analysisSemester 5, 8 ECTS (plus an optional 30-hour revision class). Basic computational algorithms: floating-point arithmetic and error analysis, nonlinear equations (bisection, Newton’s method), interpolation and approximation, numerical integration, numerical linear algebra.
  • Stochastic modellingSemester 6, 6 ECTS. Stochastic models of natural phenomena: Markov chains (in discrete and continuous time), the Poisson process, renewal processes, Brownian motion and Gaussian processes, as well as estimating model parameters from data.
  • Machine learningSemester 6, 6 ECTS.
  • Mathematical electives (categories L, M or T)At least 6 ECTS.

Specialisation courses — second cycle (curriculum from 2026/27)

  • Introduction to simulation and Monte Carlo methodsSemester 1 (February intake — semester 2), 6 ECTS. Pseudorandom number generators and their testing, simulation of random variables and stochastic processes, Monte Carlo integration, variance reduction and applications of Markov chains in simulation.
  • Differential equations 2 (extended)Semester 1 (February intake — semester 2), 6 ECTS. Modern methods of analysing ordinary and partial differential equations — dynamical systems, calculus of variations, weak solutions — and their applications in physical and biological models.
  • Functional analysis 1Semester 1 (February intake — semester 2), 7 ECTS. Basic concepts of functional analysis — the Hahn–Banach theorem, Banach and Hilbert spaces, bounded linear operators.
  • Advanced numerical methodsSemester 2 (February intake — semester 1), 6 ECTS. Constructive approximation of functions — polynomial interpolation, Gauss quadrature and fast algorithms — with hands-on computation in Python.
  • Time seriesSemester 3 (February intake — semester 4), 6 ECTS. Time series models (trend, seasonality, stationary processes), fitting models to empirical data, forecasting and spectral analysis.
  • Specialisation team projectSemester 3 (February intake — semester 2), 4 ECTS. A mathematician’s research workshop — preparing and presenting a mathematical text as part of a team.
  • Survey seminarsTwo seminars, 4 ECTS in total.
  • Course in the foundations of the exact or natural sciences (NP category)At least 4 ECTS.
  • Mathematical electivesAt least 36 ECTS in categories M or T and 11 ECTS in categories L, M or T.

Courses in the foundations of the exact or natural sciences (NP category)

  • Fundamentals of genetics6 ECTS, winter semester, every 2 years. Mechanisms of inheritance (Mendel’s laws), basics of molecular genetics and gene expression, genetic cross problems.
  • Geostatistical methods in environmental analysis4 ECTS, winter semester, every 2 years. Statistical inference and time series in environmental research with a spatial perspective — random functions, the variogram and kriging.
  • Mathematical cartography3 ECTS, summer semester, every 2 years (listed for the previous curriculum).

Recommended electives — second cycle (previous curriculum)

  • Functional analysis 1Foundations of functional analysis — Banach and Hilbert spaces, linear operators.
  • Analytic functions (extended)Complex functions and their applications, e.g. to computing improper integrals and physical quantities.
  • Mathematical foundations of quantum mechanicsThe mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics (Hilbert spaces, self-adjoint operators) and the probabilistic interpretation of the wave function.
  • Linear modelsLinear regression and analysis of variance — inference theory and practical data analysis.
  • Nonlinear functional analysisMethods of nonlinear functional analysis (fixed-point theorems, Sobolev spaces) and their applications to partial differential equations.
  • Mathematical programming and optimizationLinear and discrete programming and optimization — the simplex method, Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions, gradient methods.
  • Probability 2 (extended)Conditional expectation, martingales and other tools needed, among others, in stochastic analysis.
  • Simulations and algorithmic applications of Markov chainsSimulation of Markov chains, random walks on graphs and MCMC methods.
  • Fourier series and transformFourier series and the Fourier transform and their applications in approximation theory and differential equations.
  • Graph theoryBasic concepts, methods and theorems of graph theory, including matchings and the chromatic number.
  • Statistical inferenceAdvanced mathematical statistics — Bayesian and minimax estimation, hypothesis testing theory.
  • Introduction to applications of wavelet analysisWavelet analysis as a modern method of signal analysis — theory and implementation of basic algorithms.

Skills

  • Build simple models of natural phenomena based on differential equations, solve them and analyse the solutions.
  • Construct models of biological problems — population dynamics, epidemics, reaction–diffusion systems — and study the properties of their solutions numerically.
  • Create simple stochastic models of natural phenomena and estimate their parameters from data.
  • Choose and implement algorithms for simulating random variables and stochastic processes, assess the quality of simulations and apply variance reduction techniques.
  • Implement numerical algorithms (interpolation, approximation, numerical integration, linear algebra) and apply them to computational problems.
  • Formulate and analyse variational problems and apply dynamical systems methods to problems of classical mechanics.
  • Fit time series models to empirical data, prepare forecasts and carry out spectral analysis.
  • Design relational databases and work with them using SQL.
  • Work in a team on a mathematical project and prepare and present mathematical texts.
  • Cooperate with specialists from other fields, keeping in mind the need for empirical verification of models.

Careers

According to the curriculum (UWr Senate resolution no. 48/2026), graduates of the applied mathematics specialisation — like those of data analysis and actuarial and financial mathematics — are prepared to work in sectors of the economy that require high professional competence, critical thinking and the ability to solve non-standard problems. The curriculum stresses that graduates of specialisations such as data analysis or applied mathematics are essential in industry and in innovative start-ups, where a thorough understanding of data science tools is needed; as an example of a position for graduates of the programme it names, among others, data analyst.

First-cycle studies also provide good preparation for second-cycle studies, and the best master’s graduates can continue at the doctoral school. Employment and salary data for mathematics graduates of the University of Wrocław are published by the national Graduate Tracking System (ELA) (external site).

FAQ

When and how do I choose the applied mathematics specialisation?

In the first cycle you submit your declaration at the end of semester 4 (by 30 June) using Form C2. The plans of the AD, MAF, MS and theoretical mathematics specialisations are similar in semesters 1–4, but it is worth preparing for the decision from semester 2, for example when choosing course versions. In the second cycle you submit your declaration at the beginning of semester 1 (by 15 October) using Form C3. Deadlines and forms are listed on the programme page.

Can I change my specialisation or follow two?

In the first cycle you do not submit a binding declaration until the end of semester 4, and if you decide later, some courses assigned to semesters 2–4 can be made up in later semesters (see the notes to the semester plans in the guide). In the second cycle, with the Dean’s consent, you can change your declaration or follow two specialisations.

Do I have to choose the extended versions of courses?

The extended version of Differential equations 1 R is compulsory. The specialisation coordinator also recommends the extended version of Probability 1 R, as it makes the specialisation considerably easier. For Mathematical analysis, Linear algebra and Introduction to mathematics, choose the version that best matches your abilities; the choice of version of Algebra 1, Topology and Measure and integral has no significant bearing on this specialisation.

What do I need to know to follow applied mathematics in the second cycle?

The specialisation requires the knowledge and skills taught in Differential equations 1 R, Deterministic modelling, Stochastic modelling and Numerical analysis — all of which are part of the first-cycle plan of the specialisation. If you completed a different specialisation or degree, you can make up missing competences during your studies, but ECTS credits for such supplementary courses do not count towards the 120 ECTS required to graduate.

How can I take an NP course taught at another faculty?

You need two consents — from the dean of the faculty teaching the course to enrol (contact that faculty’s dean’s office) and from the Dean of the Faculty of Mathematics and Computer Science to count the course in the NP category. The Dean decides after consulting the specialisation coordinator, so it is advisable to obtain the coordinator’s preliminary approval before submitting the request.

Can I replace Fundamentals of practical statistics with another course?

Yes — with Statistics (semester 5) or Fundamentals of statistics (semester 6), provided this does not prevent you from meeting the semester completion requirements. However, such a swap makes it impossible to take Linear models in semester 5, and therefore Advanced linear models and Analysis of large data sets in semester 6.

Do I write a thesis in the first cycle?

No — under the curriculum from 2026/27, first-cycle studies end with a written diploma examination, which you take after completing semester 6. You prepare a (master’s) thesis in the second cycle over two semesters; you choose a supervisor at the end of semester 2 or the beginning of semester 3.

Which curriculum applies to me?

The curriculum from 2026/27 applies to students who started their (first- or second-cycle) studies in October 2026 or later. Students who started in February 2026 or earlier follow the previous curriculum; some of its courses are being phased out according to the schedule published on the programme page.
